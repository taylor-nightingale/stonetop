# Playbook moves

What they are, and what is true about them that any design has to live with.

Distinct from the moves everyone has — basic, expedition, special and follower — which the system
supplies, nobody chooses, and which are covered separately.

---

## What they are

A move a character picked from their playbook. It is the thing that makes them this character rather
than another one with the same numbers.

A move is a **rule written as a sentence about the fiction**: *when you take damage despite your best
efforts to avoid it…*, and then what happens. The sentence is the rule. There is no shorter form of
it that is still correct — the first emphasized phrase is sometimes the whole rule and sometimes
skips the part that matters, so nothing derived from the text can stand in for it.

## Scale

- A playbook offers **22 to 25**.
- A character takes two to four at creation and one at most levels after.
- They will hold **nine to twelve at the very top**, and a character that high up is rare.
- The catalogue never changes size. The part of it still open to you shrinks as you take things,
  and shrinks again where requirements gate what is left.

**So the hardest moment is the earliest one.** At second or third level nearly everything is
available and the player knows the playbook least. By ninth they hold most of what they will ever
hold and are choosing between a handful.

## What you do with them

**In play, you search your own moves.** Three questions send you there, and they are not separable:

- *Did something just fire?* Something happened and a move may cover it.
- *What have I got that could help here?* A problem, and your moves are the tools you own.
- *What exactly does this one say?* You know which move, you need its words — which of the
  following, how much, what it costs.

They interleave. You sweep your own list, something looks possible, you read it properly, it does
not apply after all, you carry on. **Knowing whether a move fired often needs the whole move box**,
not the opening clause — so the full text is part of the search rather than the thing found at the
end of it. An index that hides the words behind an action turns one question into twelve round
trips.

That is what makes this hard. It is not a lookup, where you know the name and want the entry. It is
a sweep across everything you own, against a situation, with the answer sometimes only visible in a
move's last line.

**This is what separates them from the moves everyone has.** A basic move collapses to its name and
trigger perfectly well: there are ten of them, they never change, every character has them, and
after a few sessions a player knows what Seek Insight does — the name is enough to find it again,
and the trigger confirms it. Abbreviated, a basic move is a reminder of something already known.

Playbook moves are none of those things. A player has held them for a few sessions at most, they
differ from every other playbook's, and they are not the same shape as each other: some open with a
clean *when you…*, some are standing facts about the character, some are instructions that only
matter at advancement, some have no trigger to lift at all. Abbreviated, a playbook move is not a
reminder, it is a first encounter — and a first encounter with half the sentence missing is the silent failure
above.

**But familiarity grows, and it is not uniform.** A move held for two years is as well known as a
basic one; a move taken last session is not. The same character has both — the ones they started
with are known cold, the one picked at fourth level is still being learned. So how much of a move a
reader needs is a property of their relationship to it, not of the move. It changes over time, it
differs between two players holding the same playbook, and it differs between two moves held by the
same character. Nothing in the data can tell you which is which.

And it fails silently. Reading fails visibly — you cannot find the words. A sweep that misses just
ends, and nobody learns that a move applied.

**When choosing, you survey everything on offer.** At creation and on levelling. The comparison is
now across the whole playbook: things never used against things already held, some out of reach,
some already spent.

The two want different things. Play wants all of your own, with the words reachable without going
elsewhere. Choosing wants the shape of everything, most of which will never be yours.

## What is true of them

- **Some can be taken more than once**, up to a limit the move states. Taken to that limit, a move is
  finished rather than blocked — a different fact from one you were never able to take.
- **Some require something first** — a level, or another move.
- **A character may hold a move whose requirement is not met.** The rules do not forbid it and a GM
  may grant it. Requirements describe; they do not gate.
- **A move may be granted from outside the playbook** altogether.
- **Most do not roll.** Of a playbook's own moves, roughly one in six carries a roll; the rest are
  things that are simply true, or that you spend.
- **Most track nothing.** A few carry uses that are spent and refilled.
- **A move may contain its own choices**, made when the move is taken and read with it afterwards.
- **A move may have no name.** Some are printed as a rule and nothing else.

## What owned moves are, while choosing

Not obstacles. **Reference.** Requirements point at moves a character already holds — one move
requires another — so while choosing, the moves already taken are how you read the ones you cannot.
A design that pushes them out of the way hides the answer to the question it just asked.

## What a move is attached to

A move is not only text. It can carry a roll, a track of uses, a set of choices, a requirement, and a
count of how many times it has been taken. Any of those may be absent; all of them may be present
together. Whatever presents a move has to survive the sparse case — a name and two sentences — and
the dense one without becoming two different things.
