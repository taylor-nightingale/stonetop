# Followers

The people, animals and things that go along with a character and generally do what they are asked.

---

## What they are

**A follower is an NPC.** The GM decides what they do, what they say and what happens to them, and
portrays them — for every character at the table, not only the one they follow. They sit on the GM's
side of the line, and a player treating one as a second character is grounds for stopping the game
and resetting expectations.

Three things, and only these three, separate a follower from any other NPC:

- they generally do as their character says;
- they can accumulate **Loyalty** to that character, which makes them easier to lead and manage; and
- when a character has them do something, **they can trigger player moves**.

They are usually people — often named villagers who already have a tie to the character: *your
mentor, a talented healer*, *your acolyte, beloved by birds*. Well-trained beasts, bound spirits and
sentient artifacts count too.

**Being a follower is a decision, not a property.** An NPC with a full stat block is not a follower
until the table treats them as one, and the test is whether they generally do what the character
says. A hound acquired but not yet trained is not a follower. A villager who will not simply do as
asked is not one either. The relationship can also be declined for no reason beyond not wanting the
bookkeeping.

## What one is made of

- **Tags** — words that describe them, *warrior*, *stubborn*, *tall*; they affect what is rolled.
- **Damage, HP and armor**, which work the same for followers as they do for characters.
- **An instinct**, which describes *how they tend to cause trouble for you*.
- **GM moves**, written as things they can or will do. **Not every follower has these.**
- **A cost**, which describes what keeps them following your lead.

Compact enough that other rules define a follower inline, mid-sentence, without ceremony — *treat
them as followers: group, tiny, gross, meek, stealthy; HP 1 each; Instinct: to get distracted; Cost:
genuine affection.*

Loyalty is left out of that shorthand because it never varies: it begins at 0 and tops out at 3 for
everyone.

Damage and armor are written as prose rather than as bare numbers — *walking stick 1d4 (close)*, *1
lacks organs*, *hoofs 1d6+3 (hand, close, forceful)* — and the qualifier is the mechanical part, not
decoration around a number.

## Directing one

**Telling a follower to do something has three outcomes, and only one of them is a move.**

**Agreement.** If the GM asks what the player thinks their follower would do and agrees with the
answer, no move triggers and nothing is rolled. The GM simply decides what happens. This is the
common case and it is not Ordering Followers.

**Direction.** Ordering Followers triggers only when a character *directs* a follower to do something
that would trigger a player move. Then that move fires, and the character's player rolls it — not
with a stat:

- **+1** if the follower has at least one appropriate tag or move, **+2** if they are also
  *exceptional*
- **+0** if nothing they have is relevant
- **disadvantage** if any of their tags would get in the way

**Relevant tags do not stack.** A member of a group who has both a useful group tag and a useful
personal one is still +1. Only *exceptional* reaches +2. A group directed to act as a whole is a
single roll, made on the tags the group shares.

**Refusal.** Followers will not always do as they are told. They get scared — they freeze when
surprised, flee a terrifying foe, panic in the face of horror. They also resist when they are angry,
miserable, shocked or overwhelmed; when the order is unreasonable, foolish, degrading or distasteful;
and when it cuts against their instinct, tags, cost or traits.

The refusal is told to the player, and it opens a small procedure:

- The player may spend **1 Loyalty**, if they hold any, and the follower does it anyway.
- Otherwise, if the follower would have done it willingly but for the fear, the character is having
  them **Defy Danger** — the danger being that they freeze, panic, or make a mess of it.
- If they would not have done it willingly, they have to be **convinced**: Persuade, or some other
  move, exactly as with any other NPC.
- Or the character lets it drop and does something else.

**The friction is the design, not an edge case.** A follower's fear and foibles are what make their
Loyalty worth having: one who always does as they are told and never acts up gives their character no
reason to pay their cost at all.

## Tags and GM moves

**A tag names the territory; a move names a concrete way the follower occupies it.** *Toxic* does not
say whether the toxicity arrives by bite, by touch, or by spitting. A move does.

The Dool spirit is *terrifying, stealthy, devious*, and what it does is sense a victim's doubts,
shape sound and shadow to unnerve them, and manifest as their fears — three specific ways of being
terrifying. The Mighty Servant is *large, strong, hardy, slow*, and it performs mighty feats of
strength and carries on implacably.

Neither list is recoverable from the other, and they are read together. Tags alone say what area a
follower covers and never how it happens — and *how* is what is needed to narrate the action and to
argue that the tag applies. Moves alone give particular acts with no account of the competence they
come from. Both count identically towards the roll, because either answers *does this follower bear
on the task*; only together do they answer *and what does that look like*.

**A follower's moves are what they do, not what they can be ordered to do.** They are written from
the GM's side, and a good third of them are things nobody would ever ask for: *grow bored and go to
sleep*, *run low on fuel*, *start fires, cause collateral damage*, *wander off*, *become confused,
unsure what to do*, *possess you in your sleep*. That last is printed on the same short list as
revealing secrets, on a follower a character owns.

**The same is true of everything written on a follower: it is both their competence and their
liability.** *Greedy* is a bonus when the task is haggling and a disadvantage when it is guarding
treasure. Nothing marks which is which, because it depends entirely on what is being asked, and the
GM is told to play instinct, tags and traits up as a source of trouble. Nothing about a follower can
be presented as a list of bonuses.

Riders ride on the act rather than standing alone: *manifest a ghostly presence (harmed only by
silver or salt)* is one move with terms attached to doing it.

## Loyalty is bought, and spent against their nature

Paying a follower's **cost** — when it has not been paid recently — earns 1 Loyalty, to a maximum of
3. It is the ordinary route, not the only one; moves exist that strengthen a bond as though the cost
had been paid.

Loyalty is spent one-for-one to have a follower overcome their fear, **resist acting on their own
instinct, tags or traits**, or do something they would rather not — within reason, and never the
abhorrent or the suicidal.

That makes Loyalty **the budget for having someone act against their own nature**, and it ties the
parts together: the cost says what they want, the instinct, tags and moves say what they will do if
left alone, and Loyalty is how much of that a character can override before it runs out.

The cost's timing is fictional, not scheduled. *Recently* is a judgement made at the table, and
nothing can count it down. It is often paid by doing something the fiction was going to do anyway —
letting a crew celebrate the night before the journey pays theirs.

**Loyalty is not always a bond.** A spirit that manifests holding 1 Loyalty, and a flute that
shatters if it is dismissed holding none, is using the same three points as a timer. A beast marked
and held for 3 Loyalty ends when the last point is spent.

## Instinct is how they cause you trouble

Every follower has one, and the book's phrasing is exact: an instinct *describes how they tend to
cause trouble for you*. It is not a personality note. A wolf whose instinct is *to fill its belly*
chomps the obvious bait and refuses to leave a carcass; a crew member who gambles leaves her post for
a game of dice, or brings a creditor to the door.

For some, the instinct is chosen from a list when the follower is taken — *to take needless risks*,
*to bicker, infight, and hold grudges*. For a named person from the village it is already decided and
is part of who they are.

A follower without orders, or acting on their own initiative, does what the GM says they do. A
follower is never idle while unattended.

## A group is one follower with several people inside it

A crew, a pack of hounds, vermin in the walls. The group is the follower, and the sharing is exact:
its members have **the same tags, max HP, armor, damage, instinct, GM moves and cost**, share **one
pool of Loyalty**, and each have **their own current HP**.

A group can be directed to act as a unit, or its individual members given orders of their own.

Members **start anonymous**. They become individuals through play: *when an individual member stands
out*, they get a name, a tag, and one or more traits. Six identical bodies become a particular person
with a grudge, and that happens the moment the fiction needs it — the same movement as an unnamed
mark becoming a specific item (`outfitting.md`).

**Members die, and stay listed.** A crew roster carries its dead alongside the living, because who
was lost is part of what the group now is. New people join, including NPCs who were not followers
before.

**Traits are relationships, not statistics.** Many are written with a blank in them — *crush on __*,
*grudge against __*, *__'s kid/sibling/cousin* — so filling one in writes a connection to someone
else in the game. A member's traits are what makes them worth remembering; their tags are what makes
them useful.

## What they carry

**Followers Outfit just like characters.** The same catalogue, the same counting: three or fewer
light, four to six normal, seven and up heavy, nine an advisory ceiling that nothing enforces. They
hold their own supplies, with their own uses.

This is a reason to bring one. Supplies are spent by the head — one use per person per day — so a
follower is both another mouth and another pair of hands, and what they carry is theirs rather than a
share of the character's load.

## Where they come from

A playbook grants one. So does a background, which may offer five named villagers and ask for two or
three. So do arcana, inserts, possessions and moves. Livestock can be traded for or requisitioned.

**And they arise on their own.** An NPC starts following a character around and doing what they say;
a merchant is saved and stays; a recruit asks to join a crew; a requisitioned horse turns out to
matter to somebody. A follower who arrives this way could be anyone, and the stats are settled after
the relationship, not before it.

**A follower frequently arrives as part of something else**, described in that thing's prose rather
than existing in its own right — which is why the inline shorthand exists at all.

## What is true of them

- **A follower need not be a creature.** A ring or a cloak with a will of its own is a follower: no
  HP, no damage, nothing to carry, but Loyalty, instinct, cost, tags and moves all apply.
- **A follower never rolls.** There are no dice on their side of the table; the character's player
  rolls, and the follower's qualities are what modifies it.
- **Everything about them is mutable.** Moves exist that grant a crew new tags, a larger damage die,
  or more HP each, and that re-choose their instinct and cost outright. *All starting values are
  subject to change in play* is a rule, not a caveat.
- **They can die**, and one move prices it: spend 1 of the follower's own Loyalty and they survive,
  out of the action but alive; let them go instead and the character marks experience.
- **Other currencies substitute for Loyalty.** A leader may hold a pool of their own and spend it in
  lieu of a follower's Loyalty, or to gain advantage on the order itself.
- **Nothing caps how many a character has.** A crew, a herd, a companion and a bound spirit can all
  be held at once, and one of them contains six more.
