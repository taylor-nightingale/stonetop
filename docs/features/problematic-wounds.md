# Problematic wounds

Lasting hurts that are not hit points and not debilities. The broken bone, the ruined knee, the scar.

---

## The three tiers

Convalesce is the move that names all three in one breath, which is why it is the anchor for this
whole feature:

> When you ***rest for a few days, in safety and comfort***, recover all your HP and clear all your
> debilities. When you ***rest for a few weeks under the care of a healer***, heal any problematic
> wounds that can heal. If you ***have suffered a permanent injury or impairment***, either retire or
> Make a Plan to adapt to it.

**Hit points** come back with a rest. **Debilities** clear with the same rest. **A problematic wound
does not** — it needs weeks and a healer, and only if it is the kind that can heal at all. **A
permanent injury** is not healed by anything a character can arrange; the move's own answer to having
one is to retire the character or change how they live.

That is a ladder of permanence, and each rung is a different kind of thing rather than more of the
last one.

## What a wound is

**Authored fiction, not an entry from a list.** There is no table of wounds anywhere in the data, and
nothing enumerates them. One comes into being when the fiction says so, described in whatever words
fit — the book's own examples of the permanent kind are *regrowing a limb, removing a scar, fixing a
bad knee*.

**It states no mechanical effect.** This is the sharpest difference from a debility. *Dazed* says
exactly what it does — disadvantage on +INT and +WIS. A problematic wound says nothing of the sort:
what a ruined knee stops you doing is decided in play, every time, by what is being attempted. So
nothing can compute from a wound, and nothing should try.

**It is not a condition, a poison or a disease.** The Invocations insert offers all of these as
separate picks — *"Has one of their problematic wounds stabilized"*, *"Recovers from a minor condition
(drunk, etc.)"*, *"Is cured of a dire affliction, poison, or disease"*. Neighbouring categories, not
the same one.

## The states a wound is in

Three words appear in the data, and they are three different states:

- **Active** — Redwood Basin heals *"all active problematic wounds"*, so a wound that has not been
  dealt with is in a state that can be named.
- **Stabilized** — Invocations, unempowered, gets one *"stabilized"*; empowered, the character
  *"fully recovers from a problematic wound"*. Stabilizing is explicitly less than healing.
- **Healed** — it is gone.

And running alongside, set when the wound is first suffered rather than reached later: **whether it
can heal at all.** Convalesce heals only *"problematic wounds that can heal"*, which means some
cannot, and that a wound arrives already knowing which sort it is.

## Healing is adjudicated, never computed

Recover is explicit about who decides:

> When you ***tend to a debility or a problematic wound***, say how. The GM will either say that it's
> taken care of or tell you what else is required to do so.

The player says what they are doing about it. The GM answers. There is no track to fill, no counter
to run down, and no condition a sheet could evaluate.

## Permanent injuries essentially never leave

Clearing one is an arcanum-grade miracle. Three separate arcana each offer it as one of a short list
of once-in-a-campaign effects — Creepy Cave (*"Heal yourself of a permanent injury"*), Redwood Basin
(*"Fully recover from a permanent injury by next season's end"*), Path in the Woods (*"Recover fully
from a permanent injury or impairment"*). The Mummified Hand heals problematic wounds but is not
listed as touching permanent ones.

**They also arrive in batches.** The Labyrinth ends with *"each PC must pick 1: You have suffered a
permanent injury or impairment — what and how?"* — a single scene that gives one to everybody.

**But they do not pile up without end, and the limit is the character rather than the rules.**
Convalesce gives two answers to having one — *"either retire or Make a Plan to adapt to it"* — and
adapting is a real continuation, not a stay of execution. A character carries one, perhaps two, and
goes on. Past that the fiction answers for them: there is only so much a person can adapt around, and
retirement is the move the book reaches for. So the practical ceiling is a handful, set by what is
playable.

That makes the accumulation itself worth reading. A player looking at their second permanent injury
is looking at the beginning of that decision, which is a thing the sheet should let them see coming
rather than something they discover when the GM asks.

## What is true of them

- **They outlive the rest.** HP returns in a day, a debility in a few; a wound takes weeks and a
  healer, and a permanent one takes a miracle or a retirement.
- **A wound knows at birth whether it can ever heal**, and nothing later changes that.
- **Stabilized is a real state**, short of healed, and reachable separately.
- **Nothing about a wound is mechanical.** No roll is modified, no cap applied, no number derived.
- **The GM decides what clears one**, case by case, from what the player says they are doing.
- **Permanent ones accumulate, up to a point** — a whole party can gain one at once, and a character
  carries one or two before retiring or adapting becomes the question.
- **They are read while something is being attempted** — the question a wound answers is "can I
  plausibly do this", which is asked in the middle of play, not between sessions.
- **The printed playbook has nowhere to record them.** That is a gap in the sheet, not evidence that
  the group is not tracking them.

## Not yet confirmed

**The defining passage has not been read.** Everything above is reconstructed from the eight places
in the packs that *use* the term — four moves, three arcana, one wonder — because the rules text that
introduces problematic wounds lives in Book I/II prose that has never been extracted. The tiers, the
states and the adjudication are all quoted from moves, so they are safe; what is missing is whatever
the book says directly about what counts as one and how a GM should pitch them.

Worth reading that passage before this document is relied on, and quoting it here when it is found.
