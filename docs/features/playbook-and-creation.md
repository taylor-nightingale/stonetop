# Playbook and creation

Where a character comes from, and what the choosing of one settles.

---

## What a playbook is

**A template.** A set of choices and options that point towards a specific archetype while leaving
room to make the character unique. There are nine, and **no two players take the same one** — the
party is nine kinds of person, never two of a kind.

A playbook is worked **front to back, making choices as instructed**, and then it stops: the last
section is a procedure done with the whole table, and a player who reaches it waits for everyone
else. Up to that line the work is solitary. After it, nothing is decided alone.

## What it settles

Seven choices: **background, instinct, appearance, place of origin and name, stats, playbook moves,
special possessions.**

And a set of things it records rather than asks: the damage die, starting HP, spaces for armor,
experience, level and debilities, a set of prompts that define part of the world, and the procedure
for introducing the character to the group.

Everyone starts at **level 1**, including a character joining a party that is not. The book says
plainly not to worry about the imbalance: a low-level character has fewer options, not less effect.

## What each choice carries

They are not equal, and the differences are not where a reader would guess.

**Background** is the heaviest. Three per playbook. It says how this person came to be exceptional,
and it hands over real things — a unique move, an extra playbook move, a special possession,
sometimes followers. It also decides **which world-building questions the character is asked**, since
those prompts are written per background rather than per playbook.

**Instinct is binding.** It describes how the character tends to behave, for better or worse, and
unlike the rest it is a claim about conduct rather than a capability — it says what this character
will do against their own interest. It is also the one the **end of every session asks about
directly**: demonstrate it or struggle against it and the character marks experience.

**Appearance is referenced, not binding.** It carries no numbers, which is not the same as carrying
nothing — it is a fictional cue, and the fiction is what moves trigger off. How a character looks,
sounds and carries themselves changes what they can plausibly attempt, how people treat them, and
what happens next. It is spoken aloud when the character is first introduced, and after that it is
what a player reaches for every time they describe their character doing anything, and what everyone
else pictures. Less weight than instinct; the same permanence.

**Origin** is a place on a real map. Stonetop is home, or close enough, to every character, but a
family came from somewhere and each playbook offers its own set of somewheres. *If unsure, pick
Stonetop.*

**A name** comes from the origin's list, or does not. The lists exist to give the world a cohesive
feel, not to constrain; more names live on the village's own sheet; and at least one origin is
printed with **no list at all** and the instruction to pick a name from any of the others. An empty
list is an instruction, not an omission.

**Stats, moves and possessions** are covered in `stats-and-conditions.md`, `playbook-moves.md` and
`possessions.md`. In short: an array assigned as the player likes, three or four moves, and one or
two possessions from six or seven offered.

## Creating a character writes the world

This is the part that makes creation more than a form.

The prompts on a playbook define **the world and the character's place in it**, and at the start of a
new game the answers decide what Stonetop and the lands around it actually look like for that group.
Nobody is filling in color against a fixed setting; the setting is partly being written here.

Some choices write into the village's own sheet directly: a background exists that adds named
neighbours to the steading's list, one trait chosen for each. **A character's background can change
the village.**

Joining a game already in progress inverts the obligation — then the choices have to fit what has
already been established, and the GM or another player briefs the newcomer first.

## Introductions

The last section of a playbook is a procedure for the table, run once everyone has stopped there.

Players take turns. The rule attached to it is short and absolute: **when someone reveals something
and you want to know more, ask. When someone asks you a question, answer it truthfully.**

- **First turn** — name, pronouns, background, origin and appearance.
- **Second turn** — the special possessions, and *how you contribute to the village, beyond working
  the fields*.
- **Third turn** — something the playbook chooses: the sacred pouch and its remarkable trait, a
  history of violence and what keeps you up at night, what you are worried about.
- **Fourth turn** — answer one of a set of questions, **naming one or more NPCs who live in
  Stonetop**: *who is your closest kin, whose heart and soul is entwined with yours, who would be
  lost without you.*
- **Again, or pass.** Round the table once more, until everyone has passed.
- **Last turn** — **ask the other players** one of a set of questions about their characters: *which
  one of you fears the wider world, which one of you has stayed my hand, which one of you doubts the
  power of Danu.*

The shape matters. The first three turns describe a person; the fourth and fifth **populate the
village with named people**; the last **binds the characters to each other**. A party leaves
Introductions already owing each other history.

And the decisions are deliberately held open until then: a player may read the section over and think
about answers, but **should not settle anything until the group is ready to do it together**.

## Almost nothing is locked

- **Instinct can change at any time** it feels right — to one from another playbook, or to something
  invented — as long as the table is told. Levelling prompts the question; it does not own it.
- **Appearance is a starting point.** Details get added and changed in play.
- **Alternatives can be substituted** for a background, an origin, the special possessions, or even
  playbook moves, with the GM and the whole group approving.
- **A playbook's own name can change.** One of the nine crosses a word off its title when a
  particular move is taken, and the character is thereafter something else.

## Inserts

Every playbook comes with at least one, and more can be acquired — including by dying. They carry
moves, choices and sometimes an instinct of their own. See `inserts.md`.
