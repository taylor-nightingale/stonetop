# Possessions

The things a character owns that other people in Stonetop may not.

Distinct from what a character is carrying today, which is `outfitting.md` — a possession is one of
the things a load can be made of.

---

## What they are

A possession is defined by **scarcity, not by power**. Every household in Stonetop has a cookpot, a
spear, a cloak, a pair of snowshoes; those are common, freely lent and traded, and nobody tracks
them. Few households have a sword, or parchment and ink, or an anvil and bellows. Those are
possessions.

So the set is not an account of what a character owns. It is the short account of what they own that
is **worth someone else's attention**.

## What you do with them

Six moments call on them, and only the first is a player considering their possessions as a subject
in their own right.

**Creating a character.** A playbook offers six or seven; the character takes one or two. What is
offered is mostly not adventuring kit — a smithy, a tannery, an apiary, a herb garden, a goat herd.
Creation asks the player to describe their possessions alongside *how you contribute to the village,
beyond working the fields*, and those are the same question. **Choosing possessions may be choosing
what the character does for a living**, with any gear a consequence of the trade rather than the
point of it.

**Packing for an expedition.** A possession that can be carried is among the things a character may
mark and take, and it counts against load like anything else.

**Needing something already on the road.** A mark committed but not yet named may become anything
commonly available, or **any of the character's own possessions** — provided it is something they
could have had with them all along. Possessions are a third of the answer to *what did I have with
me*, and that question is asked away from home, in the middle of something else.

**Doing what the trade knows how to do.** Owning a smithy is knowing how to smith, and knowing metal,
and knowing who else in the village works it. A possession is a claim about what a character can do
and knows, not only about what they hold: *assume that you're proficient with these items.* A few
state the capability outright — advantage when persuading domestic beasts, an extra use of provisions
when foraging. Most leave it to the fiction, which makes it no less real and no less owned.

**This is the moment that sits closest to reading your moves** (`playbook-moves.md`), not packing. A
possession and a playbook move are both standing capabilities a character holds, both answer *what
have I got that could help here*, and both are swept rather than looked up. They differ in form: a
move states its own trigger and what follows, while a possession states a fact about the world and
leaves the consequences to the table. So a possession's effect is frequently written down nowhere —
it has to be argued from, and a player arguing needs the words in front of them.

**Spending a use.** The possessions that carry a finite count are spent in the moment the rule they
belong to fires.

**Trading, being given, or giving away.** What a thing is worth, and whether the trade happens at
all.

**Five of the six arrive from somewhere else.** The question is rarely *what do I own*. It is *does
anything I own let me do this, know this, or stand here claiming I can* — asked mid-activity, under
whatever pressure the fiction is applying, about a set the player mostly did not write. The answer is
as often a licence as an object.

## How the set grows

**What creation gives is small and closed.** One or two picked, and on some playbooks one or two more
granted outright alongside them. A granted possession is not a pick and does not spend the budget.
A character finishes creation holding one to three.

**Everything afterwards is open and unbounded.** Possessions are acquired through play — as loot, as
a gift or a reward — and by trading for them. The world holds far more of them than every playbook's
list put together, so a long-running character's possessions are mostly things no playbook ever
mentioned.

**Advancement never adds one.** Levelling buys moves. The only thing it does here is raise the
ceiling of a track a character already has.

Nothing caps the total and nothing prunes it, so the set only grows.

## What is true of them

- **A possession has no single shape.** It may be the contents of a trade, a rule with a track, a
  standing bonus, a follower with its own stats, something that yields goods on a seasonal cycle, a
  fact about the character's standing, or a pointer to rules kept somewhere else entirely. Nothing
  marks which of these any one of them is, and they run from three words to a full stat block.
- **A possession is not fully described by what it grants.** Gear and tracks are the written part;
  most of what a possession does is permission — to act, to know, to be believed — and that part is
  unwritten. Reduced to its grants, a possession loses the majority of itself.
- **A possession with no mechanical effect is a full and ordinary choice.** *A heap of expectations*
  grants nothing, tracks nothing and changes no roll, and still spends a pick — what is being bought
  there is who the character is. Once capability rather than gear is understood as the common case,
  these are not the exceptions they look like: such a choice is never a lesser one, and nothing may
  read as though a pick were wasted.
- **Only part of a possession becomes gear.** One sentence names both the things that can be packed
  and counted against a load, and the things the character simply has. The second kind is fiction and
  is never tracked. Both readings of that sentence are needed, and neither replaces the other.
- **A possession may not be carryable at all** — a smithy, a herd, shelters out in the wild. Those are
  held apart from anything to do with a load.
- **Access can stand in for ownership.** Some are held *or access to it*: not the character's
  property, simply open to them.
- **A track's refill rule belongs to the possession.** By default a use spent is gone for good, the
  possession itself remaining, and getting more is something to be arranged with the GM. Every
  exception names its own trigger, its own quantity — sometimes a die roll rather than a number — and
  its own clock: a season, or a stretch of quiet time at home of the same kind levelling waits on
  (`advancement.md`). None of them can be run on a schedule.
- **A track does not always count uses.** It may hold named things instead, one to a slot, and the
  name is then the part that matters.
- **A maximum can move**, with level and with moves taken. A track's ceiling is not a constant of the
  possession.
- **A possession may carry its own choices, and they need not be made at creation** — *choose up to 3,
  now or later*.
- **A possession does not belong to the playbook that offered it.** Several are offered by more than
  one, and a character may end up with anything at all.
- **Two possessions can share a name and be different things.** They are the same possession only
  when they are the same entry in the catalogue, never because they read alike.
- **Plural means replaceable, singular means irreplaceable.** *Thick gloves* can be made or acquired
  again; *a boiled leather cuirass* is the only one, and losing it is losing it.
- **Acquiring or selling one is a roll, not a transaction.** Value runs 0 to 4 and is logarithmic —
  three Value 1 items are three Value 1 items and never a Value 3 — and trading is a move with a
  partial success on it. Common items need no roll at all.
- **The set shrinks only by giving away, trading, or discarding.**
